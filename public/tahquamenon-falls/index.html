    UPPER OR LOWER FIRST?

    Where should you begin? It depends on the day you want.

    UPPER FALLS

    Start here for the great waterfall.

    Upper Falls is the sight most visitors have come to see: a sweeping curtain of tannin-dark water spilling nearly 50 feet into the gorge. Paved approaches and accessible viewing platforms make a memorable stop possible without taking the steep stairs to the lower overlook. If you have only an hour, give that hour to this place.

    Short visitSignature viewAccessible
    LOWER FALLS

    Come here when you can linger.

    The Lower Falls are less about one grand view than a whole stretch of river. Walk the boardwalks, cross the island bridge and listen as water slips over separate rock ledges. Families and curious photographers often find themselves staying longer than planned. Parts of the area are accessible, but choose the route that fits your group.

    READ THE LANDSCAPE

    There’s more to Tahquamenon than the waterfall.

    Look closely on the walk in. The color of the water, the trees underfoot and the shape of the river all tell you something about this corner of the Upper Peninsula.

    01 · THE WATER

    Why does the river look like strong tea?

    That deep amber color is part of Tahquamenon’s character. As water travels through the forested wetlands upstream, natural tannins from decaying vegetation stain it brown. The color is not, by itself, a sign of muddy or polluted water. At Upper Falls, the river pours over a broad ledge and churns into pale foam against the darker water. Stop long enough to watch how the colors change as the light moves.

    02 · THE FOREST

    Don’t hurry through the old trees.

    The walk to Upper Falls is part of the experience. The newer accessible boardwalk bends through old-growth forest, with places to rest and notice the trunks, roots and layers of shade. This is a fine place to slow your pace before you hear the full roar of the falls. Listen for woodpeckers and watch the light shift beneath the canopy; even on a crowded day, the forest deserves a few quiet minutes.

    Boardwalk and viewing access ↗
    03 · THE TWO FALLS

    One river, two different personalities.

    Upper Falls puts the river’s power on display all at once: nearly 50 feet of drop across a ledge more than 200 feet wide. Four miles downstream, the Lower Falls break into several channels around an island. They reward a slower walk and a willingness to explore a different overlook. If you visit both, pay attention to the change in sound. A single roar becomes a collection of smaller voices.

    04 · THE LIVING PARK

    The wildlife is here even when you don’t see it.

    Beyond the busiest viewpoints, this is a great stretch of northern forest and wetland. The DNR lists beaver, otter, porcupine, pileated woodpecker and spruce grouse among the park’s wildlife, with occasional moose sightings in wet areas. There is no guarantee of an encounter. Your best chance to notice something may come when you stop talking for a moment, look for tracks or listening birds, and give animals plenty of distance.

    WINTER / XC SKIING

    When winter comes, the forest changes its voice.

    Snow softens the woods, ice forms along the river’s edges and the falls keep their powerful current beneath the winter scenery. The Upper Falls area has 4 miles of groomed cross-country ski trails, including the 1-mile Lantern Loop and 3.8-mile Giant Pines Loop. Michigan DNR asks walkers and snowshoers to stay off groomed ski tracks; check the marked winter routes and current conditions before heading out.

    BEFORE YOU SET OUT

    A few things I’d want you to know about this park.

    Give the river enough time

    With 45 minutes, stay at Upper Falls and savor the overlook. With 90 minutes, add the forest boardwalk or a stop at the Fact Shack. Three hours makes both falls comfortable. With half a day, cross onto the Lower Falls island; a full day lets you add a substantial hike or the Rivermouth. These are starting points, not guarantees about driving, parking or walking time.

    You don’t need the steep stairs for a good view

    Upper Falls has a 1,100-foot accessible boardwalk winding through old-growth forest, with landings where you can rest and look toward the river. Accessible viewing platforms are available without taking the long stairway down to the gorge. Lower Falls has accessible viewing areas and the Ronald A. Olson Island Bridge. Conditions, grades and available mobility equipment can change; call the park about the route that best suits your group.

    The River Trail is a hike, not a shortcut

    The 5.1-mile River Trail connects the falls through forest, hills, roots and uneven ground. You’ll be following the river rather than walking a smooth path between parking lots. Bring water, wear proper footwear and arrange a confirmed return ride if hiking one way. Shuttle service is seasonal; walking along M-123 is not a safe substitute for a missed ride.

    Where I’d take an inquisitive family

    Show the kids Upper Falls first if they love big sights, then give them time at Lower Falls to discover how many ways a river can fall. The island bridge and separate overlooks make a good adventure with plenty of places to pause. Keep children close to the water, stick to marked routes and use only designated wading areas when those are open.

    A meal without another drive

    The Tahquamenon Falls Brewery & Pub sits by the Upper Falls day-use area, making it an easy place to settle in after the overlook. The Lower Falls area also has a seasonal café and concession. Hours can change with the season, so check the operator before planning a meal around it. If you’re headed into the backcountry, carry your own food and water.

    Don’t underestimate winter here

    The falling water is beautiful in winter, but spray can freeze on surfaces and daylight is short. Skiers can explore the Upper Falls trails when grooming is available, while snowshoers should use appropriate routes away from ski tracks. The connecting River Trail is not maintained as a winter shortcut between the falls. Check the DNR winter map and the latest weather before starting.

    FAQ

    Questions worth asking before you arrive.

    Can I see both Upper and Lower Falls in one visit?

    Yes. The day-use areas are about four miles apart along M-123, and three hours is a sensible starting point for seeing both. Give yourself extra room for parking, photographs, the island bridge or a longer overlook visit.

    Which falls should I prioritize?

    Choose Upper Falls when time is short or you want the grand, nearly 50-foot curtain of water. Choose Lower Falls if you enjoy short walks, changing viewpoints and exploring around an island. With enough time, they tell two halves of the same river’s story.

    Do I need to hike between the falls?

    No. Drive between the day-use areas on M-123 if that suits your visit. The 5.1-mile River Trail is an optional, more demanding point-to-point hike; a one-way walk requires a verified return plan. Do not use the highway shoulder as a replacement trail.

    Is Tahquamenon worth visiting in winter?

    Yes, especially if you enjoy the hush of a snowy forest against the sound of moving water. Ice may gather along the falls, but the river stays powerful and surfaces can be slippery. Follow winter routes, keep off groomed ski tracks unless skiing, and confirm current access before traveling.
