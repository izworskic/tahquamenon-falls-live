TRUTH BOUNDARY

We separate waterfall flow from river context.

The commonly used USGS Snake River gauge near Twin Falls is downstream of Shoshone Falls. It is useful river context, but it is not the same thing as water spilling over the falls. This tool labels it that way.

WHY FLOW CHANGES

Milner Dam, irrigation and releases matter.

The City of Twin Falls says the falls are controlled largely by water-management decisions roughly 20 miles upstream at Milner Dam. Spring runoff and federal flow-augmentation releases can transform the view quickly.

WHAT A GOOD FLOW LOOK LIKE

Flow changes the entire experience.

Idaho Power has described about 2,300 cfs over the falls as enough for a spectacular display, while fall conditions can be minimal enough that the falls look nearly dry. The photo is a real Shoshone Falls image, not a live view.

PLACE CONTEXT

Falls, lake and canyon stops.

Why can Shoshone Falls look nearly dry?

Much of the Snake River is managed upstream for irrigation and reservoir operations. The City of Twin Falls notes that late-summer and fall flows can be minimal, while spring snowmelt and occasional federal flow-augmentation releases can produce much larger displays.

When is Shoshone Falls usually strongest?

Spring is normally the best season for high water. The city says a typical spring peak is roughly 10,000–12,000 cfs, with wet years exceeding 20,000 cfs. Summer and especially fall can be much lower.

How much walking is required?

The City of Twin Falls says the closest parking is about 75 feet from the main viewing platform and that multiple viewpoints are ADA accessible.

Can I camp at Shoshone Falls?

No. The city does not allow overnight camping at Shoshone Falls or Dierkes Lake.
